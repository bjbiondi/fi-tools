At $1,500 a month, about 18.0% of gross pay, with the yearly raises you entered, you land on roughly 81% of the combined targets. Being short today is not the same as missing. The money you set aside now is the money that compounds longest, so the early dollars carry more of the outcome than the gap suggests.

One version of closing it: start at the same $1,500 and raise it about 9.5% a year instead. That reaches every target above without setting aside another dollar this month.

The other levers are all still open: move the year on whichever goal is genuinely the most flexible, lower a target that was a guess to begin with, or decide that one of these goals is worth less to you than what the money is doing now. The number is here to make the tradeoff visible, not to hand down a verdict.

How this works: Each goal is solved backward from its target. What you already have keeps compounding, anything contributed from elsewhere counts toward the target first, and what remains is yours to fund. Because the amount rises each year, the number you start with is lower than a flat contribution would be. Returns are entered as real returns, meaning after inflation, so every figure stays in today's purchasing power the same way the FI Number Calculator does. A goal funded in two years should not carry the same return assumption as one funded in twenty five. Your own contributions are treated as arriving at the end of each month and rising once a year. Anything coming from elsewhere on a yearly basis is treated as a single deposit at the end of each year, so it compounds for a few months less than the same amount spread evenly would. College is treated as one amount needed in a single year rather than spread across four, and tuition has historically risen faster than general inflation, so a lower real return or a larger target is worth considering there. Every amount you set aside is rounded up to the next $25, at every size, and each total is the sum of those rounded amounts rather than a rounded sum, so the columns add up if you check them. Rounding up means the figure shown is never less than the target needs. Money coming from elsewhere appears as you entered it, since rounding that up would claim a contribution nobody is making. This is a rough landmark for your own thinking, not a forecast, and it doesn't account for taxes, employer vesting schedules, or annual contribution limits.
